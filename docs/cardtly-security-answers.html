Cardtly: security answers

Meeting crib sheet · prepared 21 September 2026 · internal

If you only get one minute

Customer data sits in Postgres behind row-level security, which means the database itself refuses to hand out a record to anyone not entitled to it, rather than trusting the website to remember to ask.

The business card is public, because that is the product. Everything behind it, the leads it captures, the account, the billing, is not.

We never see card numbers. Paystack does. And a customer can export or delete everything we hold without asking us.

The questions you will actually get

Where is our data stored?

Postgres on Supabase, in their West Europe region, hosted on Vercel. Payments run through Paystack, a South African provider.

So our data leaves South Africa?

Yes, it is processed in the European Union, and that is disclosed in our privacy policy rather than buried. POPIA section 72 permits this where the receiving jurisdiction gives adequate protection, and the EU is the benchmark that adequacy is usually measured against. GDPR is at least as strict as POPIA, not a weaker regime.

Section 72 also allows a transfer that is needed to run the service they signed up for, which covers it independently.

Name the rest yourself: our other providers can process what they receive outside the EU, including in the United States. The one to volunteer is OpenAI, which handles the two optional AI features: the bio writer, and scanning a paper business card. Section 8 of the privacy policy states all of this.

Answer this plainly and without apology. "In the EU under GDPR" is a stronger answer than most local hosting, and hesitating makes it sound like a problem when it is not. But do not say "the EU" full stop and leave the US transfer to be discovered - that is the answer that unravels.

We need data to stay in South Africa.

If that is a hard requirement, say it is a separate conversation and that you will come back with an answer rather than committing on the spot. It would mean moving the database region, which is a migration, not a setting.

Only say this if they raise it. Most private-sector buyers do not; it usually comes from public-sector tender conditions.

Who can see the leads our staff capture?

Only your account. The rule lives in the database, not in the website, so even a bug in a page could not leak them. There are 50 lead records in the system today and someone outside can read zero of them.

Is our card public?

Yes, deliberately. A business card that needs a login is not a business card. Anyone with the link sees the name, title, company, contact details and links, exactly as if they had been handed a printed one.

What is not public: who scanned it, who saved it, and what happened next. That is your data.

So what should we not put on a card?

Anything you would not print on a physical one.

What happens when an employee leaves?

The card belongs to the organisation, not the person. An admin reassigns or removes it, and the leads that card captured stay with you.

Do you store our credit card details?

No, and we could not produce them if you asked. The card is entered on Paystack's infrastructure. We hold a token that can charge the agreed subscription and nothing else.

Is it encrypted?

In transit, yes, HTTPS only and enforced, so a browser will not even attempt an insecure connection after the first visit. At rest, the database and its backups are encrypted by the platform.

Can we get our data out?

Yes, and you do it yourself. One file containing everything we hold on the account, downloaded on demand. No email, no ticket, no waiting period.

Can we delete everything?

Yes, self-service. It cascades through leads, cards, teams, organisations, orders and billing, then removes the login itself.

What about POPIA?

The access and deletion rights under sections 23 to 25 are built in and self-service rather than a promise to respond within 30 days. The published privacy policy names every sub-processor, states retention periods, and points to the Information Regulator. Breach notification under section 22 is a documented process.

Who are your sub-processors?

Supabase for the database, sign-in and file storage; Vercel for hosting; Paystack for payments; Resend for email; OpenAI for the two optional AI features; ipapi.co to look up the city a new account signed up from; Google for Wallet passes and, in the Android app only, Firebase Analytics; and Apple for the App Store. All named in section 7 of the published privacy policy, with what each one receives, which we will send you.

The OpenAI one is worth stating before they find it. The bio writer sends the job title, company, expertise, location and any existing bio a user types, only when they ask it for a draft. Card scanning sends the photograph of a paper business card someone was handed, so that is another person's contact details. Both are optional, and no stored lead or contact is ever sent to OpenAI.

Volunteer this rather than waiting to be asked. An AI sub-processor found by a reviewer who was told the list was complete costs more trust than the feature is worth.

Who at Cardtly can see our data?

A small named team, through an admin interface, and admin actions are logged. Worth saying plainly: fewer people have access here than at a large vendor with an offshore support floor.

What happens if you are breached?

Notification to the Information Regulator and to you as soon as reasonably possible, as section 22 requires. Administrative actions and every billing change are logged, so we can tell you specifically what was touched and when.

Row-level security is one setting. What if it is misconfigured?

Fair, and it was. Postgres row-level security is row level, not column level: a policy that lets somebody edit their own record lets them edit every field of it, and the platform grants that by default. So an account holder could write fields they should never touch.

That is now closed by column-level grants. A signed-in user may write the content of their own card and their own name, and nothing else. Trial dates, admin flags, subscription state, seat counts, organisation billing and analytics counters are writable only by the server. The anonymous key that ships in every page can write nothing at all, on any table.

Measured, not asserted: every table in the database was probed with the public key on 21 September 2026 and none accepted a write. A technical buyer can repeat that test themselves with the key from our page source.

What do your response headers look like?

HSTS for two years, so a browser refuses plain HTTP after the first visit. Frames blocked to same origin, MIME sniffing off, referrer trimmed to the origin cross-site, and a permissions policy that denies microphone, geolocation, USB and payment outright and allows the camera only to us, for the card scanner.

No Content-Security-Policy header yet. If they ask specifically, say so. It is the one item on this list we have not done.

Do you have multi-factor authentication?

No. Sign-in is email and password only. Passwords are stored as one-way hashes, and sign-in attempts are rate limited by the authentication platform.

Do not offer Microsoft or Google sign-in as available: neither is live. Microsoft sign-in is built but switched off, and needs an Azure app registration before it can be. If a Microsoft 365 customer wants their own tenant to enforce MFA, say it can be enabled and come back with a date. There is no Google sign-in at all. Checked on cardtly.com on 23 September 2026; an earlier version of this page said both existed.

Have you ever had an incident?

One, and it was ours rather than an attacker's. A billing record was deleted during maintenance and restored the same day. It is the reason every billing change is now logged with who did it, when, and the before and after state.

Say this if asked. Answering "no, never" to this question is rarely believed and is worth nothing if it later turns out otherwise.

What not to overclaim

Say plainly if asked No ISO 27001 and no SOC 2. Small South African company, not a certified multinational. The controls above are real regardless, and claiming a certification you do not hold is the fastest way to lose a technical buyer.

No independent penetration test. Do not imply otherwise.

No Content-Security-Policy header. The other security headers are set; this one is not.

Rate limiting is partial. The customer API enforces a per-key hourly limit and returns 429 over it, and sign-in attempts are limited by the authentication platform. The website's own internal endpoints are not separately rate limited. Say the first two and do not round it up to "yes, we rate limit".
